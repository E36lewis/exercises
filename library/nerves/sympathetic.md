The sympathetic nerve consists of a complex network of nerves, ganglia, and plexuses that run parallel to the spinal cord.
The nerve fibers originate from the intermediolateral cell column of the spinal cord, specifically from the levels T1 to L2.
The sympathetic nerve has two main components: the preganglionic fibers and the postganglionic fibers.
The preganglionic fibers emerge from the spinal cord and synapse with postganglionic neurons in autonomic ganglia, 
forming a chain of ganglia called the sympathetic trunk or chain.
From the sympathetic trunk, postganglionic fibers are distributed to various target organs and tissues throughout the body.

The sympathetic nerve is responsible for activating the body's 'fight or flight' response, which prepares the 
body for action in threatening or stressful situations.
It regulates many physiological processes, including heart rate, blood pressure, pupil dilation, sweating, and digestion.
The sympathetic nerve increases the release of stress hormones like adrenaline and noradrenaline, leading to increased 
heart rate, elevated blood pressure, and heightened alertness.
It also facilitates the dilation of bronchioles in the lungs to improve oxygen intake and redirects blood flow to the 
skeletal muscles for increased physical performance.

The sympathetic nerve fibers synapse with postganglionic neurons in autonomic ganglia located within the sympathetic trunk.
These ganglia form interconnected chains on either side of the spinal cord and extend from the base of the skull to the coccyx.
The ganglia contain collections of nerve cell bodies and serve as relay stations for transmitting signals between the 
preganglionic and postganglionic fibers.
